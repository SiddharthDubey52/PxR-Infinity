An old tweet resurfaces. A clip goes viral without context. A rumour starts on a gossip page. For a public figure in India, a crisis can go from one post to national headlines in a few hours.

How you respond in that window often decides how the story is remembered. This guide covers what to do — and what not to do.

## First, is it really a crisis?

Not every negative comment needs a response. Responding to a small issue can make it bigger. Ask:

- **How fast is it spreading?** A handful of posts or thousands?
- **Who is talking?** Random accounts, or media, brands and other celebrities?
- **Is it true?** Fully, partly, or not at all?
- **What’s at stake?** Upcoming release, brand deals, a show?

If it’s small and slowing, monitoring may be the right call. If it’s growing, act.

## The first 24 hours

### 1. Pause — don’t post in anger

The worst responses are emotional replies, deleted-and-reposted statements and arguments with trolls. Before anyone posts anything, stop.

### 2. Get the facts

What exactly was said or shown? Where did it start? Is there missing context? A clear timeline prevents a second mistake.

### 3. Decide: respond, clarify or stay silent

- **Respond** when you’re at fault. A sincere, specific apology works far better than a vague “sorry if anyone was hurt”.
- **Clarify** when it’s false or out of context — calmly, with facts, without attacking anyone.
- **Stay silent** when responding would only amplify a small issue.

### 4. One voice, one statement

Everyone — the celebrity, manager, family, team — must say the same thing, or nothing. Mixed messages become their own story.

### 5. Brief your fans

Fan clubs will react either way. Give them clear guidance: share the statement, don’t abuse critics, don’t create new fights. An organised fan base can calm a situation; an angry one can make it worse.

## The first two weeks: shift the narrative

Once the initial wave passes, the goal is to move the conversation forward:

- **Positive, genuine stories** — work, charity, collaborations — published consistently.
- **Supporter voices** — colleagues and fans sharing their experiences.
- **Media relationships** — making sure journalists have the correct facts and a statement.
- **Daily sentiment monitoring** to catch any second wave early.

## Long-term: repair what people find on Google

Months later, what shows up when someone searches your name is your reputation. Long-term repair means building strong, accurate content — interviews, features, profiles — that ranks well and tells the full story. ## Mistakes we see most often

1. **Deleting posts without explanation** — screenshots exist and deletion looks like guilt.
2. **Blaming the “social media team”** — audiences rarely buy it.
3. **Fans abusing critics** — this becomes the new headline.
4. **Disappearing completely** for months — silence lets others define you.
5. **Threatening legal action publicly** too early — it can escalate the story.

## Prepare before anything happens

The best crisis PR starts before a crisis:

- Review old posts and content.
- Agree who decides and who speaks during a crisis.
- Keep a trusted PR contact who can act within hours.
- Build goodwill and a strong media presence in calm times.
